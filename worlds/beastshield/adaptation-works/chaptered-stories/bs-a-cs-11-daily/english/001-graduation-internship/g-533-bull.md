# A Day in the Life of Niu Dazhuang

## Scene 1: The Scale of the Iron Bed

Darkness has weight.

Niu Dazhuang woke in that familiar weight. Not that the sky wasn't bright yet — the dormitory's only window was nailed shut with old burlap sacks, letting in just a few strands of iron-gray light. The air was thick enough to pull threads — twelve male beastmen packed into eight bunks, sweat, foot odor, overnight farts, cheap tobacco residue, and the fishy musk of various fur and sebum fermenting together in the stuffy heat, all stewing into one dense mass.

He lay still, first counting the heartbeat beneath his ribs. One, two, three... beating slow, heavy, like someone knocking on a muffled drum in the distance. This was the first thing he did every morning upon waking: confirm he was still alive.

From the lower bunk to the left came the fine grinding of Gou Laifu's teeth, creak-creak, like a rat gnawing wood. From the upper bunk to the right was Bao Su — not a sound, but Niu Dazhuang knew he was there. Black panther beastmen slept without even breathing loudly; it was a vigilance carved into bone. Diagonally across, Xiong Houshi's snores rose and fell, phlegmy and rhythmic.

Niu Dazhuang slowly sat up. The iron-frame bed beneath him let out a sharp groan — a groan with measurements: three parts to the left, where the bed leg was most rusted; five parts to the right, where the crossbeam had been bent last March when he twisted his back moving cargo and fell back onto the bed. This bed knew his nearly three-hundred-pound weight; each time it bore him felt like the last.

His feet probed downward. The cement floor was ice-cold, moisture creeping up through the soles. He found his liberation shoes — the uppers had split at the seams, patched with packing tape he'd scavenged from the warehouse. The adhesive had long since blackened, rubbing against the protruding bone of his outer ankle, grinding out a callus, then rubbing it raw, now a ring of hard dead flesh. He slipped them on, didn't tie the laces — the laces had snapped last month, replaced with hemp rope; the rope ends were rough, tied tight enough to cut into flesh.

Then came the green uniform. The fabric was hard as if soaked in starch. In the darkness, he could feel that one rivet was missing from the Beastshield logo on the left chest, the metal sheet curling at the edge, scratching his hand. The armpits, back, and crotch — places repeatedly soaked with sweat and air-dried — had formed a layer of salt-alkali that crinkled when touched. He pulled the pants up to his waist, securing them with an electrician's plastic cord, tied in a dead knot at the hip bone — his waist was too thick for any ready-made belt to fit.

Rustling sounds filled the room. Gou Laifu was awake, tail sweeping across the thin mattress with a soft shushing. Huang Biao was turning over on the opposite bunk, the iron bed swaying with him, muttering something indistinct from his mouth, the scar on his face invisible in the dark, but Niu Dazhuang knew exactly where it was — slanting from the left brow bone to the corner of the mouth, like a centipede crawling across his face.

The light cord was by the door. Niu Dazhuang walked over, toe kicking an empty tin can, which clattered and rolled away. He pulled the cord. The fluorescent tube flickered, struggling — once, twice, on the third try it suddenly blazed to life, casting harsh white light over the room full of sprawled bodies.

Eight bunk beds, crammed with twelve. Some bare-chested, green uniforms wadded up as pillows; some curled up, exposing old abrasion scars on the shoulder blades; Xiong Houshi lying spread-eagle, white belly rising and falling with his snores, the ring of fur around his navel matted into clumps by sweat. The smell in the air took visible shape under the light, as if you could see particles slowly churning.

Niu Dazhuang walked back to his bunk, crouched down, and dragged out the tin box from underneath. The box had no lock — locks were useless. He lifted the lid. Inside, pathetically few things: a spare uniform, folded square, washed white, the collar stitching loose; half a roll of medical tape, edges yellowed; an empty tin can holding a few rusty thumbtacks and a stub of pencil; at the bottom, a small pouch carefully sewn from old canvas.

He picked up the pouch, didn't open it, first hefting it. The weight was right. He undid the hemp cord at the mouth, fingers probing inside. The touch was first the coarse weave of the canvas, then the paper bills — Beastshield-issued lubi, largest denomination twenty, more tens and fives. He didn't need to look; the pads of his fingers could count by gliding along the edges: two twenties, four tens, three fives, and a few one-lubi notes. Eighty-five lubi total.

This was everything he'd saved in four and a half months.

His fingers lingered on those bills for a long time. The edges were frayed, stained with indeterminate grime — perhaps sweat from his hands, perhaps machine oil from moving cargo. Each one was stiff because it had been smoothed and pressed repeatedly. He took the money out, counted it again in the dim light. Eighty-five. Correct.

He pulled out five one-lubi notes, stuffed them back into the pouch, and carefully folded the remaining eighty, wrapping them in a small square of oil paper. The oil paper was torn from the paper that had wrapped pickled vegetable lumps at the cafeteria, still carrying a hint of saltiness and mustiness. Wrapped, back into the canvas pouch, the cord wound three times, two dead knots.

Then he picked up the tin lunch box — the lid was dented, crushed last month when Xiong Houshi accidentally sat on it. Opened, empty inside, the bottom stained yellow beyond washing. He put the pouch into the lunch box, closed the lid, pressed it down. The dented part fit the lid perfectly, tight as a seal.

Done. He sat on the edge of the bed, catching his breath. The sweat on his back had gone cold, sticking to his skin like a layer of icy film. He could feel his heart thumping heavily in his chest, once, then again.

"Brother Niu, counting money again?" Huang Biao's voice carried the hoarseness of just waking. He was already sitting up, orange-yellow fur sticking out in all directions, that scar showing a dark reddish flesh color under the fluorescent light.

Niu Dazhuang grunted, shoved the lunch box back into the tin, pushed it under the bed. The box scraped against the cement floor, the sound grating.

"I'd say, saving however much is useless." Huang Biao scratched his armpit, where the fur was darker, sweat stains congealed into deep yellow rings. "This hellhole, who knows which day..."

He didn't finish, but the meaning was all in the trailing note. Niu Dazhuang didn't reply. He stood up, walked to the corner by the door where everyone's washing gear was piled. His enamel cup was badly chipped, the characters "Beastshield" on it reduced to half a "shield." The toothbrush bristles were worn down to just a few strands, stuck into a sliver of leftover soap — soap scavenged from the cafeteria, worn to a thin slice, careful not to cut your mouth when brushing.

The water pipe was at the end of the corridor, shared by twelve. By the time he got there, three were already ahead of him. The water was ice-cold, carrying the taste of rust, splashing on his face with a jolt. He brushed his teeth, the bristles scraping his gums, bleeding, mixed with the minty cheap toothpaste foam, spat on the ground, red and white. Then he cupped water to splash his face, water running down his arms into the sleeves, the cuffs already frayed, threads dangling.

The sky should be bright by now. Through the gaps in the burlap sacks, the light had turned a murky grayish-white. From the warehouse came the creak of the iron door opening, and early birds chirping in the distance, faint, like something from another world.

He walked back. Gou Laifu was already dressed, crouching on the ground tying his shoelaces — real cotton laces, old but unbroken. Bao Su leaned against the doorframe, black fur sleek, green uniform worn with perfect neatness, even the cuff buttons fastened. In his hand he carried six aluminum lunch boxes, strung together with hemp rope.

"Who's fetching food today?" Gou Laifu asked, ears perked.

"Me." Bao Su said.

Niu Dazhuang nodded: "Get more rice porridge."

Bao Su didn't answer, turned and left. Footsteps so light they were nearly inaudible.

## Scene 2: Thin Soup and Sparse Water

Breakfast was eaten in the dormitory.

Six lunch boxes were set out on the square table in the middle, paint peeling, lids opened, contents identical: rice porridge so thin you could see your reflection, two grayish-brown steamed buns mixed with bran that crumbled when squeezed, a pinch of black pickled vegetable strips, looking like dried radish but salty enough to be bitter.

No one complained. Complaining was useless.

Niu Dazhuang sat down, picked up his portion. The rim of his lunch box had a dent from last month; when drinking soup he had to tilt it, or it spilled. He picked up a bun. The bun was cold, hard as a rock. He broke it open; the inside was grayer, bran granules visible. From the inner pocket of his uniform he pulled out a small glass bottle, thumb-sized, containing dark red chili sauce — his partner had stewed it herself, sent by a short-haul truck driver, five lubi delivery fee each time.

He used his fingernail to scoop out a tiny bit, smearing it on the softest part at the center of the bun. The sauce was very spicy, but fragrant — hometown sun-dried red peppers and fermented black beans, pounded together and stewed in oil. Just that nail-sized dab was enough for half a bun.

"Brother Niu, let me have a dip." Xiong Houshi leaned over, nose twitching, pink nose wet beneath the white fur.

Niu Dazhuang handed the bottle over. Xiong Houshi carefully dipped a bit with his own stubby finger, placed it on his tongue to taste, eyes squinting shut: "Fragrant! So fragrant!" Only then did he smear it on his own bun, very carefully, afraid to waste a drop.

Gou Laifu nibbled his bun in small bites, eyes darting toward Niu Dazhuang's inner pocket where the chili sauce was, throat moving slightly. He didn't ask for any; he knew how precious that stuff was.

Huang Biao ate fast, three or two mouthfuls stuffing the bun into his cheeks, jaw bulging, chewing hard, Adam's apple bobbing. Then he lifted his lunch box, poured the rice porridge down, glug-glug-glug. Finished, he set the box down with a clatter, wiped his mouth: "Fucking hell, feeding us like pigs."

"Be content." A hoarse voice came from the upper bunk — Old Qu, the oldest in the dormitory, a hedgehog beastman, quills always bristling, smell pungent. "When I first got here, not even pickled vegetables, just salt water with rice."

No one answered. Only chewing sounds, slurping sounds, and Xiong Houshi's contented grunts.

Niu Dazhuang ate slowly. The spiciness of that bit of sauce dissolved in his mouth, hot, then a trace of aftertaste fragrance. With that flavor, he could finish the whole cold, hard bun. The rice porridge had no rice to speak of, just cloudy water with an indeterminate bottom-of-the-pot taste. He drank slowly, each mouthful held for a while, letting his tongue deceive itself into sensing some thickness.

Gou Laifu finished his portion, licked the last drops from the bottom of the box, then looked at Niu Dazhuang with eager eyes: "Brother Niu, you going... to town today?"

"Mm." Niu Dazhuang said. The oil-paper packet pressed against his chest skin, through two layers of coarse cloth, he could still feel the hardness of that stack of bills.

"Then..." Gou Laifu pulled a postcard from under his pillow, the picture blurry, a landscape, edges curled. He handed it over, voice dropping, "Can you help me send this? To... to a friend of mine. His birthday."

Niu Dazhuang took it. The back of the postcard had a penciled address, characters crooked, like someone who had just learned to write. "Postage money?"

Gou Laifu's face flushed red, ears flattening back: "I... I'll pay you back next month when I get my wages. Spot me for now, okay? Brother Niu, please."

Niu Dazhuang looked at those yellow-brown eyes, bright, carrying a hint of moist pleading. He remembered the bundle wrapped in old newspaper hidden under Gou Laifu's bed, his entire savings, saying he'd open a stall in town once he saved enough. A hundred lubi a month, minus necessary expenses, how much could he save? Twenty? Thirty?

"Okay." Niu Dazhuang folded the postcard, tucked it into the same pocket as the money, "Postage, I'll cover for now."

Gou Laifu's tail wagged fiercely, ears perked up: "Thank you, Brother Niu! Really, thank you!"

Huang Biao snorted from the side: "Pup, still friends' birthdays. Save it, with that bit of money, you'll never open a shop in your lifetime."

Gou Laifu's tail immediately drooped, saying nothing.

Niu Dazhuang glanced at Huang Biao. Huang Biao turned away, fished out a flattened cigarette pack, shook it, empty, cursed, crumpled it into a ball and threw it into the corner.

Meal finished, everyone washed their lunch boxes. A short queue formed at the faucet. Niu Dazhuang was last; by his turn the water was already weak, dripping. He scrubbed the inside and outside with his fingers, the water ice-cold, stinging. Washed, he shook off the water, set the box upside down on the windowsill to dry. The cement windowsill was pitted, covered with a layer of black ash.

Six twenty-five, the whistle blew in the front courtyard. Sharp, short, like a blade slicing through the thin morning air.

Time to work.

## Scene 3: Entering the Warehouse

The warehouse doors were two heavy iron plates, painted dark green, paint peeling where the rusty red base showed through. The night-shift foreman was a middle-aged wild boar beastman, yellowed tusks, a half-smoked cigarette butt dangling from his mouth, handing the logboard to Niu Dazhuang — not because he outranked anyone, but because he was the most senior, eight years in this warehouse, literate enough.

"Same as always." The wild boar foreman's voice was muffled, phlegmy. "Two per team, three hours per patrol. Note anything unusual, don't go poking around on your own." He yawned, saliva spraying onto the logboard. "Truck coming this afternoon to pick up cargo from D-22, paperwork's on my office windowsill, go get it yourself. Alright, scram."

The night-shift workers trickled out, faces sallow, eye sockets sunken, like they'd been drained of essence. The six day-shift workers stood in the morning light, green uniforms varying in shade.

"How we splitting?" Gou Laifu asked.

"Same as always." Huang Biao said, stretching his shoulders, joints popping softly. "Me and Brother Niu, you two," he pointed at Gou Laifu and Bao Su, "Houshi and Dali."

No objections. Warehouse work, you got used to it; who you paired with didn't make much difference.

Each went to collect gear. Basically just flashlights — tin shells, heavy, batteries always weak; logboards — wooden boards clipped with a stack of rough paper, edges softened by sweat; and batons, rubber-wrapped iron cores, heads worn shiny, who knew how many fights they'd seen, or... what else they'd been used for.

Niu Dazhuang collected his things, walked with Huang Biao toward C-zone. Huang Biao walked while fishing in his pocket, pulling out a flattened cigarette pack, carefully pinching out a half-cigarette — really half, filter end blackened. He lit it, took a deep drag, smoke spilling from his nostrils and the corners of his mouth, mixing with his own odor of tobacco, sweat, and old injury balm.

"Brother Niu," Huang Biao suddenly spoke, his voice drifting a bit in the empty front courtyard, "what do you reckon these boxes actually got inside?"

Niu Dazhuang turned on his flashlight, the beam stabbing into the darkness beyond the warehouse doors: "Cargo."

"No shit." Huang Biao flicked ash, the ash falling on the cement ground, immediately scattered by the wind. "I mean, what specifically? Food? Supplies? Or... stuff that can't see the light?"

"Knowing too much does no good." Niu Dazhuang said, stepping into the warehouse.

Darkness swallowed him instantly. Not total black; a few safety lights hung high in the distance, emitting dim yellow light, barely outlining the huge, silent silhouettes of the cargo piles. But up close, the flashlight beam was like a dull knife, only cutting open a small patch of dense black before him. Familiar odors rushed up — dust, rust, the sweet rot of decaying wood, machine oil, chemicals, and some deeper, indescribable odor, like countless secrets fermenting together.

Footsteps magnified in the emptiness, each step echoing, bouncing off distant cargo piles, shattering into finer echoes, buzzing outward. The air was much cooler than outside, dampness wrapping dust, burrowing into the nostrils.

C-zone was all heavy stuff. Excavator track plates, bundles of rebar, machine tool parts covered with canvas, crouching in the darkness like a herd of beasts. Their task was to check seals and securing ropes. The seals were Beastshield special-issue paper, stamped with a red seal, pasted on box seams or binding points. The securing ropes were thick hemp, some already frayed and fuzzy, but the knots were tied dead.

Niu Dazhuang checked slowly. His hands were large, palms thick, pads rough, but fingertips sensitive. He pinched the edge of a seal, gently rubbing with his finger pads, feeling the texture of the paper, the凹凸 of the stamp impression, whether the glue bond at the edge was smooth. Sometimes a seal looked intact, but the glue was old, a light peel and the whole thing came off — that was the trace of someone tampering and re-pasting. He'd encountered it before, reported it, then nothing came of it. Later he learned to be smart: just note it down, don't make a fuss.

Huang Biao wasn't as careful. He swept the flashlight beam haphazardly, poked here and there with his baton, listening to the echoes. A dull echo meant solid cargo; a hollow echo meant possible problems. He poked hard, baton head knocking against wooden boxes with dull "thud-thud" sounds, especially jarring in the silence.

"Brother Niu, here." Huang Biao suddenly lowered his voice, flashlight beam fixed on a dark stain at the corner of a box on the bottom layer of a cargo pile.

Niu Dazhuang walked over, crouched down. The flashlight beam converged. The wooden box corner had a palm-sized dark stain, darker than the surrounding wood, as if soaked by some liquid and dried. He leaned in to smell. Dust, pine wood, and... a trace of extremely faint, sweet, fishy metallic smell. No, not metallic. Blood. Dried blood.

He extended his index finger, gently scraping the stained area with his nail. The surface was slightly sticky, scraping off a bit of dark red powder. He crushed it between his fingers; the powder was very fine.

"Note it." He said.

Huang Biao pulled the logboard from his back pocket, scribbled with the stubby pencil: C-17 zone, lower left box corner, dark red stain, sticky, suspected dried liquid.

"Report it?" Huang Biao asked.

"Note it first." Niu Dazhuang stood up, dusted off his hands. "After patrol, all at once."

That was the rule. You didn't run to the foreman making a fuss over every little anomaly; that got you yelled at for being "troublesome," "trying to slack off." Save them up, bundle a few together, report them all at once — showed you were doing your job, without being too annoying. Whether anyone cared after you reported it wasn't your concern.

The two continued forward. The flashlight beam cut a trembling path of light in the darkness; beyond the light, black closed in again. Niu Dazhuang's back began to sweat. Not from exertion, but the physiological reaction of staying tense for too long. The inner lining of the green uniform rubbed against his skin, sweaty spots cold, unsweaty spots stifling. He could feel his heart thumping steadily in his thick chest, thud, thud, thud, merging with the rhythm of his footsteps.

"Brother Niu," Huang Biao's voice drifted in the darkness, sounding very close and very far, "you ever think, if one day something happens here... what do we do?"

Niu Dazhuang didn't stop walking: "What happens?"

"Anything could happen." Huang Biao took a deep drag, the red tip of the cigarette flaring bright in the darkness. "Cargo explodes, someone breaks in, or... the higher-ups decide we know too much."

Niu Dazhuang didn't answer. The flashlight beam swept across a batch of newly arrived cargo. Army-green metal boxes, stacked neat, seals fresh and red. He stopped, looked closely at the seals. Stamp clear, edges sharp, no problem. But something felt off.

He leaned in to smell. Cold metallic odor, fresh paint pungency, and... a trace of extremely faint sweetness. Not sugar-sweet, but a thicker, cloying sweetness, mixed in the paint smell, almost completely covered. But he smelled it.

"Note this too." He said.

Huang Biao leaned over, nose twitching: "What smell?"

"Can't say." Niu Dazhuang straightened up. "Just note it."

Huang Biao muttered, adding another line to the logboard. Pencil characters looked gray and dim in the weak light.

They kept walking. The darkness seemed endless, the cargo-pile-formed passages winding like a maze. Niu Dazhuang's soles began to hurt. Not tired, but the pain of standing and walking for too long, the hard cement floor transmitting cold upward, soles cold and numb, like stepping on ice. He stamped his feet, the sound exploding in the emptiness, carrying far, bouncing back.

Huang Biao laughed, the laugh distorting in the darkness: "Brother Niu, you nervous too?"

"Not nervous." Niu Dazhuang said, kept walking.

But actually, a little. Not scared of ghosts, but of other things. Of what might be hiding in this darkness, of what unknown things were inside those boxes, of Beastshield's unspoken but mandatory rules, of that feeling that you could lose your job — or your life — over the slightest thing. That feeling was like a shadow, shrunken at your feet by day, swelling at night to fill the whole warehouse.

Finished checking a batch of cargo, the two stopped to rest by a thick cement pillar. Niu Dazhuang leaned against it; the pillar was ice-cold, transmitting through the sweat-soaked uniform to his back, making him shiver. He pulled out his canteen, plastic, filled with boiled water from the morning. Took a sip, the water was cold too, sliding down his throat, cold all the way to his stomach.

Huang Biao leaned against the opposite cargo box, smoking that cigarette down to the end, filter burning his fingers before he flicked it away. The butt fell on the cement ground, the dark red ember flashing a few times, then dying.

"Brother Niu," he suddenly asked very seriously, voice so low it was almost inaudible, "if something really happens, you gonna run?"

Niu Dazhuang looked at him. The flashlight beam from below lit Huang Biao's face half bright, half dark, that scar twisting in the shadows, alive.

"Run where?" Niu Dazhuang asked back.

"Dunno. Just run." Huang Biao said. "Can't just wait to die, right?"

Niu Dazhuang didn't answer. He screwed the canteen cap back on. The plastic cap had a crack; when tightened it poked his hand, he had to be careful to avoid it.

Rested enough, they continued. The darkness was still there, the road was still there. The flashlight beam split the darkness, then let it close behind. Footsteps, breathing, the occasional click of the flashlight switch. Besides that, only dead silence.

Until from deep in the darkness came a slight sound.

Very faint, like something scraping a metal surface, or like a small pebble rolling down.

Niu Dazhuang immediately stopped, flashlight beam sweeping toward the sound. The beam pierced the darkness, illuminating only emptiness, piles of cargo boxes casting long, distorted shadows in the light.

"Rat, probably." Huang Biao said, but his voice was a little tight.

Niu Dazhuang didn't speak. He listened for a few seconds. No more sounds. He gripped his baton tighter, the rubber handle sticky with sweat.

He remembered Huang Biao's words: Can't just wait to die, right?

But sometimes, you didn't even get the chance to run. Like now, in this boundless darkness, you didn't even know where the enemy was, where could you run?

Could only keep walking. Step by step, using the flashlight beam to split the darkness, using his nose to smell, his eyes to see, his ears to listen.

Then pray that today, like yesterday, would pass without incident.

## Scene 4: Midday Dust

Coming out of C-zone, the sun was already scorching.

The front courtyard cement ground was a blinding white, reflecting light so bright it hurt the eyes. Niu Dazhuang squinted, walked with Huang Biao to the faucet. The water was pumped from underground, ice-cold to the bone. He cupped water to wash his face, the water hitting his face with a jolt, skin tightening, then a little relief. He washed his face, neck, then stuck his arms under the stream. The water washed away the salt of dried sweat, leaving water trails on the dark brown skin, instantly evaporated by the heat.

Gou Laifu and Bao Su also came out of A-zone. Gou Laifu was panting with his tongue out, ears completely drooping, fur dark and light patches from sweat. Bao Su looked fresher, but black fur was plastered to his scalp, nose tip glistening.

"Any anomalies?" Niu Dazhuang asked.

Gou Laifu shook his head, tail wagging listlessly: "That batch of wooden boxes in B-zone, smell's still weird. Brother Bao's marker hasn't been moved."

Bao Su nodded, said nothing.

Xiong Houshi and Xiong Dali came out last. Both bears were broiling; Xiong Houshi stuck his furry head directly under the faucet, white fur soaked, water spraying everywhere. Xiong Dali just cupped water to drink, thick fingers not closing tight, most of the water leaking out.

Lunchtime. The cafeteria cart wheeled over, wheels creaking. The pusher was a chubby raccoon beastman uncle, apron shiny with grease.

Lunch was a hair better than breakfast. Besides thin porridge and buns, there was a spoonful of watery stewed cabbage, floating two fingernail-sized slices of fatty meat. Everyone also got a boiled egg — Wednesday's fixed benefit.

Niu Dazhuang took his portion, walked to the shade at the back wall of the warehouse and sat down. There were ants at the base of the wall, marching in a long line carrying unknown food crumbs. He carefully shifted his butt, not crushing them.

The egg was warm, a little hot in his palm. He carefully peeled it; the shell was brittle, peeling with fine crackling sounds. The white was smooth, the yolk cooked just right, edges with a faint gray-green ring from overcooking. He broke it in half, ate it with the bland cabbage. The cabbage had no flavor, salt miserly, stewed to mush, chopsticks pinching and it fell apart.

Gou Laifu came over, sat next to him. The little dog beastman didn't eat his egg right away, but held it in his palm to warm it.

"Brother Niu," he asked quietly, eyes darting toward Niu Dazhuang's chest — where the money and postcard were pressed against his skin, "the post office... open at noon, right?"

"Open." Niu Dazhuang said.

"Then..." Gou Laifu held out the egg, "this, for you. As... as a delivery fee."

Niu Dazhuang looked at that egg. The shell peeled clean, the white gleaming softly in the noon light. He knew Gou Laifu looked forward to these few days each month for an egg; normally the cafeteria didn't even have a whiff of grease.

"Eat it yourself." Niu Dazhuang pushed his hand away, "Growing body."

"I'm not a kid anymore." Gou Laifu muttered, but didn't insist, took the egg back, nibbling in small bites. Each bite, eyes squinting shut, chewing carefully, as if tasting some delicacy.

Huang Biao snorted from the side, he had already swallowed his egg in three bites, using a bun to wipe the vegetable oil from his lunch box: "Pup, treating one egg like treasure. Wait till you really open a shop, eat eggs every day, till you puke."

Gou Laifu's ears flattened back, said nothing, kept his head down eating.

Bao Su not far away ate quietly. He peeled his egg with extreme patience, the white unbroken, then put the whole thing in his mouth, chewing slowly. Eating cabbage too, one chopstick picking up just a little, silently delivering to his mouth. Finished, he took his lunch box to the faucet to rinse, inside and out washed clean, then inverted on the stone platform to dry.

Xiong Houshi and Xiong Dali were competing who ate faster, two bear heads pressed together, slurping sounds overlapping. Xiong Houshi had vegetable leaves at the corner of his mouth, Xiong Dali laughed foolishly.

Niu Dazhuang finished the last bite of bun, drank the thin soup from his lunch box too. He stood up, dusted the dirt from his butt.

"I'm off." He said.

"Brother Niu, go safe." Gou Laifu said, tail wagging slightly.

Huang Biao leaned against the wall, eyes closed, whether sleeping or awake unclear, just grunted through his nose.

Niu Dazhuang crossed the front courtyard, pushed open the small iron gate. The iron gate was badly rusted, pushing open with a grating screech. Outside was a dirt road, baked white by the sun, stepping on it soft and fluffy, floating dust burying the foot surface. On both sides were waist-high wild grass, green turning black, cicadas screaming desperately in the deep grass, the sound continuous, baking people's hearts anxious.

He didn't walk fast. Two hours for the noon break, four li there and back, send something, time was plenty. He even wished the road was longer, so he could stay outside a little longer.

The warehouse grew smaller behind him, finally completely blocked by wild grass and dirt slopes. Niu Dazhuang stopped, looked back. Only the rust-red roof peak was still visible, silently reflecting in the blazing sun.

He turned, kept walking. The dirt road turned a corner, a few low farmhouses appeared ahead, mud walls, thatched roofs, crooked and tilted. A bony stray dog lay under the eaves panting, saw him, lazily lifted an eyelid, then drooped it down again.

Further ahead, the outline of the town appeared. A dozen or so gray houses huddled together, the tallest only two stories. The post office was at the far east end of town, a lone single-story building, the green paint on the walls peeling in patches, exposing the yellow mud base underneath.

Niu Dazhuang pushed the door in, the bell above the doorframe jingling.

Inside was much cooler than outside, smelling of old paper, paste, and dust mixed together. Behind the counter sat an old goat beastman, wearing reading glasses with one broken leg tied to his ear with hemp rope, dozing off, head bobbing.

Hearing the bell, the old goat looked up, cloudy eyes staring at Niu Dazhuang for quite a while, before slowly pushing over a mailing form.

Niu Dazhuang took the form, pulled the oil-paper-wrapped stack of money from his chest, and Gou Laifu's postcard. He filled out the form first, writing large, stroke by stroke, very forcefully, pencil tip nearly poking through the rough paper several times. The address he knew by heart, writing without thinking.

Done, he handed the form and the oil-paper packet over together.

The old goat opened the oil paper, movements slow as slow-motion film. Inside was the eighty lubi. The old goat dipped his finger in saliva, began counting the money. Fingers trembling, bills peeled one by one, held up to the light to look, then set down. Counted very slowly, very carefully.

Niu Dazhuang held his breath, watching that stack of money turn in the old goat's hands. That was everything he had saved in four and a half months, each time resisting buying cigarettes, resisting buying snacks, not going to the town's shabby teahouse on rest days to drink the cheapest bowl of tea, saved bit by bit. Every bill was soaked with concrete sacrifices.

Eighty. The amount was right.

The old goat nodded, fumbled under the counter for a wooden box, locked the money inside. The lock was brass, small, the tongue snapping back with a crisp click.

Then weighing, affixing postage. The stamps were Beastshield internal employee special-issue, pattern a simplified logo, a bit cheaper than market rate. The old goat brushed paste on the back of the envelope, movements unsteady, paste getting on his fingers, sticky.

Niu Dazhuang watched the envelope and postcard being thrown into the green tin mailbox by the door. Thud, thud, two dull sounds. The mailbox surface was pitted, paint peeling badly, exposing the rust underneath.

"Postcard, postage money." The old goat said, voice dry and flat.

Niu Dazhuang pulled the five one-lubi notes from his chest, handed one over. The old goat gave back a few dirty coins. Niu Dazhuang took them, the coins still carrying the old goat's hand temperature, damp and sticky.

Done, he didn't leave right away. Stood in the post office, enjoying this moment of coolness and quiet. The old electric fan on the counter creaked as it turned, blades thick with dust, the wind blowing out carrying an oil and dust smell.

The old goat had dozed off again, head bobbing once more.

Niu Dazhuang turned, pushed the door out. The bell rang again.

Outside the sun was blazing, stinging his eyes so he couldn't open them. He stood at the door for a few seconds, waiting for his eyes to adjust. Then walked back along the same road. Footsteps slower than when he came.

Passing those few farmhouses, he saw a small figure squatting at the door. A juvenile rabbit beastman, gray fur, long ears drooping behind, using a stick to scratch something on the ground. Seeing Niu Dazhuang, the little rabbit looked up, red eyes curiously staring at him.

Niu Dazhuang stopped, hand probing his pocket, found a piece of fruit hard candy. The candy was from last month's cafeteria distribution, two pieces a month, he'd never been willing to eat them, the candy paper in his pocket warmed soft by body heat.

He walked over, crouched down, held the candy out.

The little rabbit looked at the candy, then at him, timidly reached out a small hand to take it, said quietly: "Thank you, uncle."

"No need." Niu Dazhuang said, voice sounding a bit coarse even to himself. He reached out wanting to pat the little beastman's head, hand halfway there stopped — his own hands were dirty, all dust and sweat. He withdrew his hand, stood up.

The little rabbit had already lowered his head, clumsily starting to peel the candy paper, long ears swaying gently with the movement.

Niu Dazhuang watched a couple of glances, then continued walking. The candy was gone, but his heart seemed a little lighter. Just a little.

Back at the warehouse, the noon break wasn't over yet. In the dormitory, some were asleep, snores rising and falling; some lay staring blankly at the yellow water stains on the ceiling from rain. Niu Dazhuang walked lightly to his bed, took off his shoes, lay down.

The bed board was hard, pressing against the bones of his back. He closed his eyes, ears full of various sounds: snores, teeth-grinding, distant faint cicada calls, and the buzzing of his own blood flowing past his ears.

He thought of that eighty lubi. Now they were locked in the post office's wooden box, in the coming days they would pass through some people's hands, finally reaching his partner. His partner would carefully open the oil paper, count once, then count again, then put it away safe. Maybe take out a little to buy some meat, or get medicine for Father. The rest, carefully hidden away, waiting for the next time of need.

Four and a half months. His next goal was to save another eighty, or a hundred. How long would that take? Five months? Six months?

He didn't know. Could only save, bit by bit.

He fell asleep. No dreams, just black, deep, like falling into very deep water.

Until the whistle blew.

## Scene 5: The Secret of the Box

The afternoon work was unloading and loading.

A blue truck waited, engine idling, emitting a low rumble, exhaust pipe puffing faint black smoke, mixing in the scorching air to form distorted heat waves. The driver was a hyena beastman, leaning against the door smoking, saw them come out, lifted his chin, considered that a greeting.

The pickup slip was on the foreman's office windowsill. Niu Dazhuang went to get it. The windowsill was thick with dust; a finger swipe left a trace. The slip was a rough piece of paper, characters printed in carbon copy: D-zone, numbers D-22 to D-25, twenty boxes of "hardware parts." Also a batch of newly arrived "machinery equipment" to be put in storage.

The six split into two groups. Niu Dazhuang, Xiong Houshi, Xiong Dali responsible for moving out the boxes to be picked up. Gou Laifu, Bao Su, Huang Biao responsible for counting and supervising loading, then receiving new cargo.

Niu Dazhuang walked into the warehouse, headed for D-zone. D-zone was at the deepest part of the warehouse, darker, colder, the smell more complex. Here were all the heaviest stuff: metal parts, machinery components, bundles of rebar. The boxes were bigger than B-zone, heavier.

Niu Dazhuang bent down, arms through the gaps at the bottom of a box, lifted it. The box left the ground, his leg muscles tensed, pant legs pulled tight, outlining the contours of his thigh muscles. Back muscles bulged, the soaked green uniform stretched into clear lines, sweat forming dark water stains under the fabric. Weight pressed on his arms, bones making slight cracking sounds. He stepped, walked toward the truck stopped in the sunlight at the door. Footsteps heavy, each step leaving a dull thud on the cement ground.

Trip after trip, trip after trip... By the tenth box, sweat was already pouring down like water. Forehead sweat flowed into his eyes, stinging. He had to stop, wipe his face with his sleeve. The sleeve was long soaked, wiping only made it stickier.

Xiong Houshi had more strength, could carry two boxes at a time, but he moved slow, careful, afraid of dropping them. Xiong Dali followed behind, one box per trip, footsteps solid, but breathing growing rougher.

Loading side, Gou Laifu was very careful, checking the seal of every box before it went on the truck, matching the number, ticking off on the slip. Bao Su didn't speak, but eyes watched every step, which box wasn't sitting steady, he reached out and straightened it with a push. Huang Biao was somewhat distracted, leaning against the truck bed, staring at the distant wilderness in a daze.

All moved out, past three in the afternoon. The sun slanted, stretching their shadows long, cast on the scorching cement ground. Six people sat or leaned in the shade, panting, no one speaking. Only heavy breathing, and Gou Laifu's tail occasionally sweeping the ground with a soft shushing.

The hyena driver signed, jumped in the cab, started the engine. The truck rumbled, slowly drove out the warehouse gate, kicking up a skyful of dust.

The dust lingered long, the air full of diesel and dry earth smell.

"Work's done?" Gou Laifu asked weakly, lying flat on the bit of shade remaining on the cement ground, belly rising and falling with breath.

"Last patrol round." Niu Dazhuang stood up, legs a little soft. He dusted his butt, "Hurry, check and we can rest."

Still two per team, each to their own zone. The afternoon patrol was quicker, mainly checking if anything got messed up from the moving, seals broken, securing ropes loose.

Niu Dazhuang and Huang Biao walked into C-zone again. Darkness swallowed them once more. The flashlight beam split the darkness, illuminating the same silent cargo piles. Passing by the wooden box with the bloodstain from the morning, Niu Dazhuang specifically shone the light on it again. The stain was still there, color seeming deeper, like congealed blood scab. He didn't touch it.

Checking that batch of metal boxes with the sweet smell, both stopped.

Huang Biao listened for a moment, lowered his voice: "Still sound."

Niu Dazhuang heard it too. Very faint, dripping sound. Like water droplets slowly, rhythmically falling on metal plate. Coming from deep within that pile of metal boxes.

The two slowly approached. Flashlight beam converged, shining on the box surface. Army-green paint gleamed coldly under the light, seals intact. But the dripping sound did exist, continuous, rhythmic.

Niu Dazhuang crouched, ear pressed to the ground. The cement ground was ice-cold, pressed against his ear. The dripping sound was clearer, carrying a hollow echo, like a clock walking somewhere far away.

"Leaking?" Huang Biao asked.

Niu Dazhuang shook his head. Didn't know. He had no authority to open the boxes, couldn't even get too close. Could only note it down.

He added another line to the logboard: C-23 zone, metal box pile, internal continuous dripping sound, rhythmic.

Done, he looked at Huang Biao. Huang Biao's face showed no expression, but there was something in his eyes Niu Dazhuang couldn't quite read — like weariness, or like some long-expected numbness.

They continued the patrol. The dripping sound was gradually left behind, but Niu Dazhuang kept feeling it was still ringing in his ears, drop by drop, knocking on something, also knocking on his taut nerves.

Coming out of C-zone, the sky was already dyed orange-red. Sunset was like spilled paint, spreading from the west all the way to the zenith. The warehouse tin roof lost its daytime glare, turning a gloomy dark red. The barbed wire was only a black silhouette in the dusk, spikes pointing skyward, silent and sharp.

Front courtyard, the night shift people were already arriving one after another, yawning, faces exhausted. Handover was simple, logboard handed over, a few words of anomalies verbally, that was it.

The six day-shift workers gathered together, waiting for the final dismissal.

Gou Laifu came to Niu Dazhuang's side, asked quietly: "Brother Niu, the postcard... sent?"

"Sent." Niu Dazhuang said.

"Postage money, I'll definitely pay you back next month." Gou Laifu said, tail restless with small wags, "Really."

"Mm." Niu Dazhuang responded. He didn't expect Gou Laifu to actually pay it back; that kid couldn't save a few coins each month.

Huang Biao walked over, patted Niu Dazhuang's shoulder, not lightly: "Off then, Brother Niu. See you tomorrow."

"See you tomorrow."

The six dispersed, each back to the dormitory. Niu Dazhuang walked last, looked back at the warehouse. The big door was being slowly closed by the night shift people, the iron doors meeting with a heavy, drawn-out grinding sound, finally clicking locked.

The warehouse was like a beast that had swallowed all secrets, falling silent in the thickening dusk, waiting for night to fully descend.

## Scene 6: Shadows Under the Oil Lamp

Dinner was almost the same as breakfast. Thin porridge, hard buns, pickled vegetables. Just the porridge was thinner, the buns colder.

Niu Dazhuang ate slowly, tasteless as chewing wax. The chili sauce bottle was already empty, the last dab at noon on the bun. Now he could only eat dry. One bite of bun, one sip of porridge, chewing mechanically, swallowing.

The dormitory lit a dim oil lamp — electricity only supplied until eight at night, after that only this. The lamp wick burned black smoke, a patch of black stain smoked onto the ceiling. The light was dim yellow, barely illuminating a few tired faces.

Gou Laifu lay on his bunk, reading his tattered *Shop Opening Guide* by lamplight, finger pointing at characters, lips moving silently. Huang Biao lay on his bed, hands behind his head, staring at the swaying lamp shadows on the ceiling. Bao Su in the corner, carefully wiping his baton with an old cloth, movements gentle as if handling some treasure. Xiong Houshi was already asleep, snores even. Xiong Dali washing his feet, the foot basin water turbid beyond description.

Niu Dazhuang finished eating, washed his lunch box, then dragged out the tin box from under the bed. He took out paper and pen — paper was from last month's wages, the back blank; pencil was just a stub, wrapped with cloth strips to hold.

He sat by the oil lamp, began writing a letter. To the one at home.

Pen tip scratched across the rough paper surface.

"Money sent, eighty. Should arrive in a few days. You put it away safe, first get medicine for Father, don't stop. The rest, see if the roof needs patching, or buy some meat, you've gotten thin."

He stopped, thought. What else to write? Every day was the same, nothing new to tell. Say he found bloodstains on a box today? Say he heard a strange dripping sound? Couldn't say. Couldn't let family worry.

"I'm fine here. Work's not hard, food's enough. Coworkers look out for me."

This lie he told himself was numb. Pen tip kept moving:

"You said the chili plants are growing well, that's good. Next time someone brings something, send me some more sauce, just a little."

"Don't work too hard. Field work that can't be finished, leave it, wait till I get back."

"That's all for now. Getting dark, lights out soon."

"Take care of your health."

He stopped, looked at those characters. Characters large, filling the paper, strokes clumsy but straight and level. He wanted to write more, but his mind was empty. Finally, at the end he drew a simple circle, representing all is well.

Folded the letter paper, stuffed it into an old envelope — envelope was reused from last time, flipped over to write the new address. Postage wait till next month's wages to buy.

Done, he blew out the oil lamp. The dormitory sank into darkness, only a bit of hazy starlight leaking in through the window.

He lay on the bed, the board hard, pressing against the bones of his back. He listened to the sounds in the room: snores, teeth-grinding, Gou Laifu turning pages with a soft rustle, Huang Biao's suppressed faint sigh, the distant faint barking of stray dogs.

He thought of that dripping sound. What was leaking? Chemicals? Or something else?

He thought of that bloodstain. Whose blood? How did it get there?

He thought of Huang Biao's words: Can't just wait to die, right.

He turned over in the darkness, the iron bed groaned. The bunk next door, Huang Biao wasn't asleep either, Niu Dazhuang could hear his suppressed, faint sigh.

He thought of home again. Thought of his partner's hands that never washed clean of dirt, thought of Father's hunched back when coughing, thought of the chili plant at home, red and hanging in the yard.

Then he stopped thinking. Too much thinking was useless, only kept you awake.

He closed his eyes, began counting how many boxes he moved today. One, two, three... counting, consciousness began to blur.

Darkness grew thicker, heavier, like warm thick water, wrapping him, pulling him down.

Before sinking completely into sleep, his last thought was: tomorrow, still have to get up early.

Still have to put on this sweat-soaked-then-dried, dried-then-sweat-soaked green uniform.

Still have to walk into that darkness-swallowing-everything warehouse.

Still have to move those boxes with unknown contents.

Days were just like this.

One day, then another.

Cycling endlessly, without end.

Until...

He didn't think further. Breathing grew even, heavy.

He fell asleep.

Outside the window, night was like ink. The warehouse slept in the darkness, like a huge secret.

And tomorrow, the sun would rise as usual.

(A Day in the Life of Niu Dazhuang · End)
