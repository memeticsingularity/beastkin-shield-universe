# Xiong Dali's Day

## Scene-1 The Awakening of Pressed Pain

Five-oh-seven.

Xiong Dali woke punctually from the chronic tenderness at the third rib on his left side—not a dream, not a memory, but the crack buried in the bone using the same method every morning to remind him: you're still alive, it still hurts.

He lay still, first waiting for that pain to shift from sharp to dull. Three and a half years ago, that warehouse accident, collapsed shelves broke three ribs, two of which healed relatively flat, only this third rib, the fracture position was刁钻, leaving a tiny misalignment during healing. Hurt on rainy days, hurt sleeping on hard board beds, hurt waking up in the morning. The doctor said "might accompany for life," saying it with an expression as calm as saying "it's raining today."

Pain stabilized. Xiong Dali then began breathing—very slow, very deep, using the diaphragm to control airflow, not letting the chest cavity expand excessively. He could feel the broken area slightly shifting with breath, like two unaligned porcelain pieces rubbing against each other.

Then he opened his eyes.

Darkness, but for someone who grew up in mountain forests like him, darkness had layers. He could see the grain direction of the wood on the ceiling, could see the green uniform hanging on the opposite bunk swaying slightly in the darkness, could see his own hand resting on the blanket—stubby fingers, thick yellow nails, finger joints larger than ordinary beastkin by a circle, traces left by long-term moving heavy objects.

He didn't get up immediately. First listened.

The dormitory was still sunk at the bottom of sleep. Niu Dazhuang was snoring, sound deep and even, like distant rolling thunder; Gou Laifu was grinding his teeth, fine crunching sounds like a squirrel gnawing nuts; Huang Biao turned over, iron bed frame emitting sharp groans; Bao Su's side was completely silent, but Xiong Dali knew he was there—black panther beastkin slept with even breathing restrained, a vigilance etched into his bones; Xiong Houshi was on the opposite lower bunk, breathing deep, occasionally making small swallowing sounds.

Safe. Everyone was still here.

Xiong Dali began moving his body, starting from toes. Not stretching, but awakening—like awakening a sleeping heavy machine. Toes slowly curled, spread inside socks, feeling rough cotton fiber friction against thick pads on soles. Then calves, gastrocnemius gently tensing, muscle fibers under skin like strings being pulled straight. Thighs, quadriceps contracting, knee making fine cracking sounds—this was also an old injury, right knee cartilage磨损.

He did these very slowly, very carefully, like disassembling a fragile bomb. The accident three and a half years ago didn't just break ribs, multiple soft tissue injuries throughout the body, mild lumbar disc herniation, right ankle habitual sprain. His body became a ruin requiring careful maintenance.

Finally, he propped up his upper body with his elbow. Movement was very slow, giving muscles ample time to relay. First triceps exerting force, then latissimus dorsi, finally waist and abdomen core tightening. Every link couldn't be wrong, wrong a bit, old injuries would protest with pain.

When sitting up, he panted. Not tired, but tense—every large movement was like gambling, gambling whether those old injuries would suddenly flare up today.

He sat on the bed edge, two thick legs hanging down, soles broad and thick, like two palm-leaf fans, nearly filling the space under the bed. Morning light hadn't come in yet, only iron-gray微光 leaking through gaps in the window's broken burlap. He looked down at his own hands—palms broad, palm calluses thick enough to be shoe soles, finger joints deformed from chronic exertion, slightly bending inward.

These hands could lift three-hundred-jin cargo cases, could bend steel bars, could smash wooden boards with one punch. But also these hands, on that accident day, couldn't support the collapsed shelves.

He shook his head, throwing off these thoughts. Can't think, thinking is useless.

Time to get up. He bent down, movements clumsy like a bear just learning to stand on hind legs. Dragged out his washbasin from under the bed—extra-large, bottom rusted through with two holes, patched from inside with tin foil, barely usable. Toothbrush was standard issue, looking like a toothpick in his hand. Toothpaste squeezed out chalk-white, carrying chemical fragrance's pungency.

He stood up with the basin. This stand, the entire dormitory's sense of space changed. He was two meters thirty tall, weight over three hundred fifty jin, when standing up his head nearly touched the ceiling, shoulders broad enough to block most of the aisle. He had to turn sideways, inhale and pull in his abdomen, to squeeze out. Passing Niu Dazhuang's bunk, he carefully lifted his foot, but knee stiffness made his movements uncoordinated, foot still scraped the bed frame, making a刺耳 friction sound.

Niu Dazhuang grunted, didn't wake.

Two people were already queuing at the faucet. Xiong Dali queued at the end, quietly waiting. He wasn't impatient, he had plenty of time—or rather, he only had time. While queuing, he observed his own body. Left wrist was faintly hurting, twisted yesterday while moving cargo; right scapula下方 muscle was tight, old injury warning that it might rain today; left ankle was somewhat swollen, from standing too long yesterday.

He listed them in his mind: wrist wear护腕, shoulder hot compress, ankle reduce weight-bearing. This was his daily homework—managing pain, allocating stamina, letting this scarred body last another day.

When it was his turn, water was already very small, dripping, like the urine of a dying patient. He collected half a basin, squatting on the stone step at the doorway to brush teeth. Squatting was very strenuous—knees couldn't fully bend, had to half-squat, center of gravity shifted back, like doing horse stance. The stone step groaned under his massive frame.

Toothbrush moved back and forth in his mouth, hard bristles scraping gums, a bit painful. He brushed very seriously, every tooth attended to. After the accident he got periodontitis, doctor said related to long-term painkiller use. Now he always brushed especially carefully, afraid of bleeding, afraid of infection—in this place, getting sick was a luxury, no one would take care of you, medical fees still had to be paid yourself.

Finished brushing, he cupped water to wash his face. Water was ice-cold, patting on face made him suck in a cold breath. Cold stimulation made him much more awake, but also made joint pain more obvious. He haphazardly wiped a few times, water flowing down neck into collar, making him shudder.

Finished washing, he splashed water against the wall base. Water hit the dirt, making dull噗噗 sounds, splashed mud spots stained his pants legs. He looked down at those mud spots, looked for several seconds, then patted with his hand—couldn't pat off, mud had already seeped into the fabric.

Whatever. Pants were already dirty anyway.

Just then Bao Su returned with six lunch boxes, strung on hemp rope, clinking. Breakfast time.

Xiong Dali touched his stomach. Not hungry, but had to eat. Body needed fuel, even if it was the lowest quality fuel.

## Scene-2 The Difficulty of Chewing

Lunch boxes arranged on the central square table, contents unchanged for ten years: rice soup thin enough to reflect a person's shadow, two bran-mixed grayish steamed buns, a pinch of black pickled vegetable strips. Today was Wednesday, each person also had a boiled egg.

Xiong Dali collected his portion—his lunch box was extra-large, an extra scoop of porridge, an extra bun. This was special care from the cafeteria uncle, because he was the largest, and because after that accident three years ago, the uncle felt sorry for him.

He carried his lunch box to his spot—the lower bunk by the door, where there was more space, he could stretch his legs. Sitting down, the bed board groaned in pain, deeply凹陷. He placed the lunch box on his thick thighs, thick thigh muscles solid, like two flat rocks, steadily supporting the lunch box.

He didn't eat immediately. First looked.

Buns were cold and hard, surface cracked, like drought-stricken land. He picked one up, trying to break it open—fingers exerting force, joints white, but bun didn't move. Finally he had to stuff the whole thing into his mouth. Bun too large, stuffing his cheeks to bulge, masseter muscles tensing, temple veins bulging.

Chewing was very strenuous. Not because the bun was hard, but because his right temporomandibular joint also had problems—hit by flying wood chips during the accident, though not fractured, but left chronic inflammation. Now every time he chewed, deep in his right cheek came dull pain, like someone lightly hammering bones.

He slowed down, using left side teeth to chew. One, two, three... each one careful, avoiding the painful area. Chewing movements looked somewhat strange—mouth only moving to the left, like chewing something shameful.

Gou Laifu came over, sitting beside him. "Brother Dali, your tooth hurting again today?"

Xiong Dali nodded, mouth stuffed with bun, speaking indistinctly: "Yeah."

"I have some painkillers here," Gou Laifu pulled out a small paper package from inside his shirt, "Leftover from last time when the clinic prescribed after injury, still a few pills. You want?"

Xiong Dali shook his head. He knew Gou Laifu's little medicine was precious, saved for emergencies. His own painkillers were long finished, no money to buy new ones—Beastshield's medical insurance only covered work injury treatment, not chronic pain management.

"You keep it," he swallowed the bun, Adam's apple rolling roughly, "I'm fine."

Niu Dazhuang walked over, handing over half a bottle of chili paste: "Dali, want some dip? Spicy opens appetite."

Xiong Dali hesitated. His stomach wasn't good either, a problem left after the accident, eating spicy would cause heartburn. But looking at that red oily chili paste, he still couldn't help extending his finger, dipping just a tiny bit, smearing it on the heart of his bun.

Chili paste was very spicy, but fragrant. It was the taste of hometown sun-dried red peppers and fermented black beans pounded together then oil-fried. Just that little bit, and the whole bun came alive. He carefully chewed, letting spiciness diffuse in his mouth, stimulating numb taste buds.

Delicious. Really delicious.

He hummed contentedly, tail patting the bed board lightly—brown bear tails were very short, hidden in thick rump fur, but still wagged unconsciously when happy.

Huang Biao sneered nearby: "Silly big guy, a little chili paste and you're this happy."

Xiong Dali ignored him. He was used to it. Buried his head and continued eating, using left side teeth to grind bit by bit, like a cow chewing cud.

Finished buns and pickled vegetables, he lifted his lunch box to drink rice soup. Rice soup had few rice grains, just cloudy water carrying a hint of scorched pot-bottom taste. He drank slowly, small sips, afraid of choking—after the accident his swallowing function was also affected, sometimes suddenly coughing.

Finally, time to eat the egg.

He carefully picked up the egg, holding it in his palm. Egg was warm, carrying residual heat of the cafeteria's large pot. He peeled it carefully, movements clumsy but earnest. Thick fingers pinching small eggshell, peeling bit by bit, afraid of breaking the egg white. Finished peeling, whole egg smooth and intact, egg white glowing with soft white luster in the morning light.

He looked at the egg, looked for several seconds. Then he didn't stuff the whole thing into his mouth, but broke it open, first eating egg white, then yolk. Egg white smooth and tender, yolk dense and creamy, carrying faint fishy and salty taste. He ate very slowly, chewing more than twenty times per bite, letting nutrients fully absorb—his body needed all the nutrition it could get, to maintain this damaged machine's operation.

Gou Laifu watched him eat, saying softly: "Brother Dali, you eat like you're taking medicine."

Xiong Dali was stunned, then smiled honestly: "Yeah. Have to eat carefully."

Bao Su was eating quietly not far away. He peeled eggs with extreme patience, egg white unbroken, then put the whole thing in his mouth, chewing slowly. Xiong Dali watched Bao Su, feeling a bit envious—Bao Su's body was intact, healthy, doing everything so smooth, so elegant.

And he, even eating was like completing a difficult task.

After eating, everyone washed their lunch boxes. Xiong Dali moved slowly, by the time he reached the faucet the water was already very small, almost dripping out. He collected some water, rubbing the inside and outside of the lunch box with his fingers. Aluminum lunch box in his thick hands looked small, like a toy. Finished washing, he shook off water—strength not controlled well, water droplets splashing in all directions, splashing the person next to him.

"Fuck! You got no eyes!" next to him was Huang Biao, splashed with water all over his face, immediately angry.

Xiong Dali lowered his head, saying softly: "Sorry."

"Sorry my ass!" Huang Biao cursed, but looking at Xiong Dali's massive frame, ultimately didn't dare to make a move, just glaring at him fiercely and leaving.

Xiong Dali stood in place, hand gripping the lunch box, finger joints white. He wanted to say I didn't mean it, wanted to say I tried to control my strength, but I couldn't control it well. After the accident, his nerve control ability was damaged, sometimes suddenly losing strength, sometimes using too much force. Doctor said this was part of post-traumatic stress reaction, brain and body connection had malfunctioned.

But he didn't say. Said it and no one would understand.

He placed the lunch box upside down on the windowsill to dry. The cement windowsill surface was pressed deeply凹陷 by the weight of his lunch box, making fine cracking sounds.

The whistle blew. Sharp and short, like a blade slicing through the thin morning air.

Time to work.

## Scene-3 Shadows in the Warehouse Depths

Warehouse Area D was Xiong Dali and Niu Dazhuang's assigned zone.

Pushing open the heavy iron door, darkness wrapped in familiar scents surged out. Xiong Dali took a deep breath, nose twitching—his sense of smell was also affected after the accident, became迟钝, many subtle smells couldn't be detected anymore. Doctor said it might be a sequela of mild cranial concussion.

He flicked on his flashlight. Beam stabbed into darkness, illuminating cargo boxes stacked to the ceiling ahead. Cases were the largest in the warehouse, all metal cases and thick wooden crates, stacked neatly, like a group of silent steel giants. Area D was heavy cargo zone, storing all the heaviest things.

"Same as usual?" Niu Dazhuang asked.

"Yeah." Xiong Dali nodded. Voice somewhat hoarse—this was also a sequela, vocal cords damaged, speaking voice always carried a bit of hoarseness.

The two walked one after another into the alleyways formed by the cargo piles. Niu Dazhuang took the lead, Xiong Dali followed behind. His gait was somewhat staggering—right leg was zero point five centimeters shorter than left leg, left from pelvic fracture healing unevenness. Normally not obvious, but in the darkness, on the uneven concrete floor, this tiny difference was magnified, making him walk like he was limping.

He walked very slowly, very carefully. Flashlight beam swayed in the darkness, beam sweeping across case surfaces, illuminating labels, numbers, seals on them. But his eyes were also affected after the accident—right eye vision declined, had mild double vision, seeing things sometimes had ghost images. He had to constantly adjust focus, squint, to see those small characters clearly.

Reaching a batch of metal cases, Niu Dazhuang stopped.

"Dali, come here."

Xiong Dali walked over. The two had to check the securing ropes at the bottom of the cases. Niu Dazhuang crouched down, Xiong Dali also tried to crouch—knees couldn't fully bend, he could only half-kneel, right knee on ground, left leg supporting. This posture made his old injuries begin to protest, lumbar spine transmitting needle-like pain.

He clenched his teeth, not making a sound. Flashlight beam shone toward the bottom of the case. Securing ropes were thick hemp ropes, tied very tight, but one place was worn severely, fibers fraying.

"Here," Niu Dazhuang pointed at that worn place, "Have to record it."

Xiong Dali pulled out the record board from inside his shirt—his was specially made, one size larger than normal, otherwise his thick fingers couldn't hold the pencil. He wrote crookedly with a short pencil: Area D-4, case 3 bottom securing rope worn, suggest replacement.

When writing, his hand was shaking. Not from fear, but nerve damage—after the accident his hands would uncontrollably tremble from time to time, fine movements especially difficult. Pencil handwriting was messy and潦草, like a elementary school student's.

Finished writing, he wanted to stand up, but waist couldn't exert force. Tried twice, neither successful. Finally Niu Dazhuang reached out and pulled him up.

"Thanks," Xiong Dali said softly, voice hoarse.

"Your waist acting up again?" Niu Dazhuang asked.

"Yeah. Old problem."

Niu Dazhuang looked at him, gaze carrying something complex—not sympathy, but some deeper understanding. Niu Dazhuang also had old injuries on his body, though not as severe as his, but understood the taste of pain.

"Slow down," Niu Dazhuang said.

The two continued patrolling. Xiong Dali's pace became even slower, each step like walking on a tightrope, carefully distributing weight, avoiding painful points. Sweat seeped from forehead, not from heat, but pain-induced cold sweat. He could feel old injuries collectively protesting: lumbar spine's stabbing pain, knee's dull pain, rib's tenderness, wrist's soreness.

But he didn't stop. Couldn't stop. Stopped and there'd be no food to eat.

Reaching the depths of Area D, he suddenly stopped.

Not because he saw something, but because he felt it—an extremely subtle vibration, coming from the soles of his feet. Very light, light enough to almost think it was an illusion. But he was especially sensitive to vibrations after the accident, any slight vibration would be amplified by him, resonating in his body.

"Wait," he said.

Niu Dazhuang stopped, turning to look at him: "What?"

Xiong Dali didn't speak, crouching down—this time left knee on ground, right leg supporting, changing posture to make waist more comfortable. He pressed his palm to the ground. Concrete floor was ice-cold and rough, palm could feel fine graininess.

Vibration was still there. Very regular, one, one, like some heavy machinery operating in the distance, or... like a heartbeat.

"There's vibration," he said.

Niu Dazhuang also crouched down, pressing his hand to the ground, carefully feeling. "No," he said, "Are you feeling wrong?"

Xiong Dali didn't argue. He knew his senses often went wrong after the accident—sometimes heard sounds that didn't exist, sometimes felt vibrations that didn't exist, doctor said this was nervous system disorder.

But he was certain this time wasn't an illusion. Vibration was very real, coming from the palm, traveling up his arm, resonating in the cracks of old injuries.

"Record it," he said.

Niu Dazhuang hesitated, but still pulled out the record board to record: Area D-11, Xiong Dali reports weak ground vibration, source unknown.

Finished writing, Niu Dazhuang looked at him: "You okay? Complexion doesn't look good."

Xiong Dali shook his head, wanted to stand up, but this time even the half-kneeling posture couldn't be maintained. Waist suddenly sent a burst of intense pain, like being electrocuted, his whole person pitched forward, hands supporting the ground to avoid falling.

"Fuck..." he cursed in a low voice, voice hoarse.

Niu Dazhuang quickly supported him: "Can't do it don't force it. Go rest for a bit."

Xiong Dali didn't逞强. He knew his limits. Under Niu Dazhuang's support, he walked to a cement pillar, leaning against it to sit down. Pillar was ice-cold, transmitting through the sweat-soaked uniform to his back, making pain slightly ease a bit.

He fumbled out his water flask from inside his shirt—specially made extra-large, could hold three liters. When unscrewing the cap, hand shook badly, water spilling out some. He tilted his head and drank a few mouthfuls, water warm, carrying plastic container's taste.

Niu Dazhuang sat beside him, also drinking water. The two were silent for a while.

"Dali," Niu Dazhuang suddenly spoke, "That accident of yours... what exactly happened?"

Xiong Dali was stunned. Three and a half years, never had anyone asked him for details. Everyone only knew warehouse shelves collapsed, he was crushed underneath, broke three ribs, multiple fractures throughout the body, lay for three months. Then after that, he became like this—limping when walking, hands shaking, eyes not good, body full of illness.

"Just... shelves collapsed," he simply said.

"I know," Niu Dazhuang said, "But why did they collapse? That day there was no earthquake, no wind."

Xiong Dali fell silent. Why? He also didn't know. That day he was patrolling Area D, walking to that row of shelves when they suddenly began shaking, then like dominoes, one row after another collapsed. He remembered turning to run, but knee old injury flared up, didn't run away. Then was darkness, pain, and ambulance sirens.

Accident investigation said "structural aging, natural collapse." But he always felt something was off. That row of shelves was new, used less than half a year. And before collapsing, he smelled a strange odor—sweet fishiness, mixed with chemical sting, very similar to the smell occasionally smelled in the warehouse now.

But he didn't say. Said it and it was useless. Beastshield's accident investigations were always perfunctory, if someone died just pay some money, if someone was injured just give some medical fees. Pursuing truth? That was asking for trouble.

"Don't know," he finally said, "Probably just bad luck."

Niu Dazhuang glanced at him, didn't ask again. The two continued in silence.

Rested enough, Xiong Dali tried to stand up. Waist still hurt, but tolerable. He supported himself on the pillar, slowly straightening up, movements like a rusty crane.

"Can do it?" Niu Dazhuang asked.

"Can," Xiong Dali nodded.

The two continued patrolling. But Xiong Dali's mind was no longer there. He thought of that day's smell, thought of the vibration just felt, thought of various abnormalities discovered in the warehouse these three years—blood stains, strange liquids, sweet fishy smells, and those cases with brand new seals but suspicious contents.

A thought suddenly popped up: maybe that accident wasn't an accident.

He was startled by his own thought, quickly shaking his head to throw it off. Can't think wildly. In Beastshield, the less you know, the longer you live.

Reaching the end of Area D, light appeared ahead—it was the passage to Area A, where there was a safety light. Dim yellow light seeped over from there, casting a fuzzy halo on the ground.

Xiong Dali turned off his flashlight. Eyes adapted for a moment to the slightly brighter light. He glanced at his record board, two abnormalities already recorded: securing rope worn, ground vibration.

"Almost done," Niu Dazhuang said, "Let's head back."

Xiong Dali nodded. The two turned around, walking back along the way they came. The return journey he walked even slower, each step like stepping on knife points.

Reaching the warehouse main gate, morning sunlight leaked in through the door crack, forming a dazzling band of light. Xiong Dali pushed the door open, light instantly flooding in, like a tide drowning them.

Outside, the sky was already fully bright.

## Scene-4 The Painful Noon Break

Coming out of the warehouse, the sun was at its fiercest.

The front courtyard concrete ground was blinding white, reflecting harsh light. Xiong Dali squinted his eyes—right eye's double vision was more severe in strong light, seeing everything with ghost images. He had to cover his right eye with his hand, using only left eye to see the road.

He and Niu Dazhuang walked to the faucet. Water was pumped from underground, ice-cold and bone-piercing. Xiong Dali directly stuck his furry head under the faucet to rinse. Water rushing on scalp made him shudder, but then came relief—cold could temporarily numb pain.

He washed face, neck, then pulled open his uniform front, letting water rush into his chest. Water flowed down dense brown fur, washing away sweat salt, leaving water traces on brown fur. He could feel water beads rolling over those old scars—chest had a seventeen-centimeter surgical scar, from thoracotomy; left shoulder had an arc-shaped scar, from steel plate removal incision; right knee had densely packed stitch marks, like a centipede crawling there.

Finished rinsing, he walked to the shade at the warehouse's back wall, sitting down with a thud. Concrete floor made a dull sound. He sat against the wall, fumbling out a small bottle of medicinal oil from inside his shirt—self-made from herbs soaking, treating symptoms not root cause, but could slightly ease pain.

He unscrewed the cap, medicinal oil's pungent smell floated out. He poured medicinal oil into his palm, rubbing it hot, then lifted his clothes, smearing it on his waist. Palm rubbing hard on skin, medicinal oil seeping in, bringing a fiery burning sensation. Pain slightly retreated a bit in the burning, like tide temporarily receding.

Finished smearing waist, he smeared knee, wrist, shoulder. Every old injury place was carefully smeared, like oiling an old machine.

Niu Dazhuang sat beside him, watching his movements, not speaking. Waiting until Xiong Dali finished smearing, he then spoke: "You smearing like this every day, isn't a solution."

"What else can be done?" Xiong Dali smiled bitterly, "No money to see a doctor."

"I heard there's an old Chinese medicine doctor in town, good at treating bone injuries, doesn't charge much."

Xiong Dali shook his head. Not that he didn't believe, but that he didn't dare. Going to see a doctor meant asking for leave, asking for leave meant deducting money. He only got a hundred lubi per month now, food and clothing deducted, the rest all bought the cheapest painkillers and plasters. Where was there money to see a Chinese medicine doctor?

Lunchtime arrived. Cafeteria food cart pushed over, wheels creaking.

Lunch was slightly better than breakfast. Besides thin porridge and buns, there was a spoonful of watery stewed cabbage, floating two fingernail-sized slices of fatty meat. Each person also had a boiled egg.

Xiong Dali collected his portion—extra-large. He carried his lunch box walking back to sit by the wall. Didn't eat immediately, first took out painkillers—the cheapest kind, side effects large, but cheap. He poured out two pills, swallowing them with porridge. Pills stuck in throat, he swallowed hard, Adam's apple rolling, face flushing red.

Gou Laifu carried his lunch box coming over, sitting down beside him. "Brother Dali, you're taking medicine again."

"Yeah," Xiong Dali simply responded.

"This medicine taken too much hurts stomach," Gou Laifu said softly, "My ma used to take this medicine before, later stomach bled."

Xiong Dali didn't speak. He knew it hurt stomach, but what could be done? When pain flared up, only wanted pain to stop, who still cared about the future.

He began eating. First ate buns, using left side teeth to slowly grind. Chewing时 right cheek deep pain came again, he slowed down, each one careful. With cabbage, he finished three buns. Cabbage had little flavor, salt was stingy, stewed mushy, but he ate very seriously—vegetables were rare, could supplement some vitamins.

Finally, time to eat the egg. But he didn't plan to eat the egg today. He carefully wrapped the egg, tucking it into his shirt.

"You're not eating?" Gou Laifu asked.

"Saving it," Xiong Dali said, "Eat it tonight."

Actually was saving it. Eggs could supplement protein, very important for this scarred body of his. He couldn't bear to finish it in one meal, had to split into two meals, even three meals.

Huang Biao came over, grass stem dangling from his mouth. "Yo, silly big guy, smearing medicine again today. Whole body medicine smell, stinking people to death."

Xiong Dali ignored him, lowering his head to pack up his lunch box.

Huang Biao stared at the egg in his shirt: "Not eating the egg? Not eating, give it to me."

Xiong Dali held the egg tight: "I'm eating. Saving it for tonight."

"Stingy," Huang Biao sneered, "A lousy egg, treating it like treasure."

Xiong Dali still didn't make a sound. He knew arguing with Huang Biao was useless, would only invite more mockery. He stood up, carrying his lunch box to wash at the faucet. Standing up, waist sent another burst of stabbing pain, body swayed, nearly falling.

Huang Biao watched his staggering back, sneering again.

While washing his lunch box, Xiong Dali ran into Bao Su. Bao Su was carefully scrubbing his own lunch box, movements smooth and elegant.

"Dali," Bao Su greeted, "Waist acting up again?"

Xiong Dali nodded, not speaking. He always felt a bit inferior in front of Bao Su—Bao Su was intact, healthy, while he was a defective product.

"I have some plasters over there," Bao Su said, "Will give you tonight."

Xiong Dali was stunned, then shook his head: "No need. You keep it."

"I can't use them," Bao Su said, voice calm, "You need them more."

Xiong Dali still wanted to decline, but Bao Su had already turned and left. He stood in place, hand gripping the lunch box, heart feeling a bit complex. Grateful, but also ashamed—he didn't want to be pitied, especially by someone like Bao Su.

Finished washing his lunch box, he placed it upside down on the windowsill to dry. The cement windowsill surface was pressed deeply凹陷 by the weight of his lunch box.

Then walked back to the wall, leaning against it to sit down. Two hours for noon break, he could rest a bit. He closed his eyes, but didn't sleep. Pain didn't allow him to sleep—waist hurting, knee hurting, ribs hurting, wrist hurting. Various pains were like a group of noisy neighbors, throwing a party in his body.

He could only adjust breathing, trying to ignore pain with willpower. Inhale, count to four; hold breath, count to seven; exhale, count to eight. This was the breathing method the doctor taught him, said it could help relax, ease pain.

A bit useful, but not much.

He thought of his hometown. Thought of mountain forests, thought of streams, thought of himself before getting injured. Back then he could climb three mountains in one breath, could break branches as thick as bowl mouths with bare hands, could carry two-hundred-jin prey and walk ten li without panting.

Now? Now he couldn't even crouch without someone helping him up.

Sometimes he would think, if that day he wasn't patrolling Area D, if that day he asked for leave, if that day he ran a bit faster... would everything be different?

But there were no ifs. Fact was, he was here, body full of injuries, every day waking in pain, working in pain, sleeping in pain. Fact was, he might be like this for the rest of his life.

A thought suddenly popped up: maybe dying would be better.

He was startled by his own thought, quickly opening his eyes. Can't think these things. Thought them and he really wouldn't be able to keep going.

He looked around. Niu Dazhuang was dozing leaning against the wall not far away; Gou Laifu was looking at his "Shop Opening Guide" under the shade of a tree; Bao Su was standing at the warehouse door, figure straight; Huang Biao was leaning against the main gate smoking; Xiong Houshi was already asleep, snores even.

Everyone was here, everyone was still alive. That was enough.

He closed his eyes again. This time he didn't think of his hometown, thought of now. Thought of tonight's dinner—maybe could eat half an egg; thought of tonight's plaster—given by Bao Su, should be more effective than his own medicinal oil; thought of tomorrow's weather—hoped it wouldn't rain, rainy days old injuries would hurt more.

Thinking about it, pain seemed less sharp. Not that it really didn't hurt, but he was used to it, or rather, he learned to coexist with pain.

The whistle blew. Sharp and short.

Xiong Dali opened his eyes, slowly standing up. Waist still hurt, but tolerable. He patted the dust from his buttocks, walking toward the assembly point.

Afternoon work was unloading and loading cargo.

## Scene-5 The Test of Weight

A blue truck waited in the center of the courtyard, engine not turned off, idling with a low rumble. Driver was a hyena beastkin, leaning against the door smoking, seeing them come out, lifted his chin.

The delivery slip was on the foreman office windowsill. Bao Su went to get it. Xiong Dali and Niu Dazhuang stood beside the truck waiting. Xiong Dali moved his shoulders and arms, preparing for the upcoming heavy physical labor. He could feel old injuries warning—waist tightening, knee souring, wrist numbing.

But he had no choice. This was his job, the only thing he knew how to do.

Bao Su returned with the slip. Area D, numbers D-22 to D-25, twenty cases of "hardware parts." Also a batch of newly arrived "mechanical equipment" to be stored.

The six divided into two groups. Niu Dazhuang, Xiong Houshi, Xiong Dali were responsible for moving out the cases to be picked up. Gou Laifu, Bao Su, Huang Biao were responsible for inventory checking and supervising loading, then receiving new cargo.

Xiong Dali walked into the warehouse, heading to Area D. Each step was careful, avoiding painful points. His right leg was shorter than left leg, walking body would slightly tilt, center of gravity unstable. On flat roads it was okay, but in the warehouse with this uneven concrete floor, each step was like walking a tightrope.

Reaching Area D, found the cases to be moved. Cases were very large, very heavy, at least two hundred fifty jin. Xiong Dali crouched down—this time he learned to be smart, first kneeling on one knee, then using both hands to embrace the bottom of the case. Took a deep breath, legs exerting force, waist and abdomen tightening, arms straining.

Case left the ground.

The instant weight pressed on arms and shoulders, old injuries collectively screamed. Waist like being stabbed by a knife, knee like being hammered, wrist like being needled. He clenched his teeth, forehead veins bulging, sweat instantly gushing out.

But he didn't let go. Couldn't let go. Let go and there'd be no food to eat.

He stepped forward, walking toward the truck stopped in the sunlight outside the door. Footsteps staggering, like a drunkard. Each step leaving heavy dull sounds on the concrete floor. His heartbeat crazily accelerated, breathing rough like a broken blower. Sweat flowed into his eyes, stinging painfully, but he couldn't spare a hand to wipe.

One step, two steps, three steps... reaching the truck, he lifted the case, pushing it into the truck bed. Putting it down, he exhaled deeply, white mist instantly dissipating in the scorching air.

Then turned around, went back to move the next one.

Second case, third case... each one was like torture. Pain spread from local to whole body, like wildfire. His consciousness began to blur, vision darkening, ears buzzing. But he still didn't stop. Couldn't stop. Stopped was admitting defeat, admitting he was a useless person.

He counted: four, five, six... each one moved, scratching a mark in his heart. This was his ritual, his way of fighting pain—using numbers to divide pain, into manageable small pieces.

Reaching the eighth case, an accident happened.

"Dali!" Niu Dazhuang and Xiong Houshi rushed over.

Xiong Dali lay on the ground, unable to move. Right knee hurt piercingly, wrist sprained, shoulder old injury recurred. He tried moving his fingers, fingers not obeying.

"You okay?" Niu Dazhuang crouched down, anxiously asking.

Xiong Dali opened his mouth, wanted to speak, but couldn't make a sound. Pain had robbed him of his language ability.

Bao Su also came over, glanced at the situation, calmly said: "Don't move him. Go call the infirmary."

Gou Laifu ran to call people. Xiong Dali lay on the ground, looking at the ceiling. Dust danced in the light, like countless tiny lives. He could feel pain raging in his body, like a group of thugs smashing and looting.

But he was strangely calm. Even a bit relieved—finally, could rest.

Infirmary people came, an old goat beastkin, wearing a white coat, wearing reading glasses. He examined Xiong Dali's injuries, shaking his head: "Right knee old injury recurred, wrist sprained, shoulder possibly fractured. Have to send to hospital."

Xiong Dali heard this, suddenly struggling: "No... don't go to hospital..."

"Don't move!" the old goat pressed him, "With injuries like this, how can you not go to hospital?"

"No money..." Xiong Dali said hoarsely, "I have no money..."

Infirmary was free, but hospital wasn't. Going to hospital, examination fees, treatment fees, medicine fees, all astronomical prices. He couldn't afford it.

The old goat looked at him, sighing: "Then you at least have to rest. These injuries can't do work anymore."

"I can do it..." Xiong Dali still wanted to struggle, but was pressed down.

In the end, he was carried to the infirmary bed. Niu Dazhuang and Xiong Houshi helped him move the remaining cases. He didn't finish the afternoon's work.

He lay on the infirmary bed, looking at the ceiling. Bedsheet was rough white cloth, with disinfectant smell. Right knee was immobilized, wrist wrapped in bandage, shoulder plastered with medicinal plaster. Pain slightly eased under medication, but still buzzing in the background, like a radio that could never be turned off.

He thought of the feeling before falling— that instant when knee suddenly lost support, that out-of-control falling fear. Exactly the same as three and a half years ago.

A thought suddenly became clear: maybe that accident, also wasn't an accident.

Maybe just like today, it was some part of the body suddenly collapsing, old injuries finally breaking under heavy weight. Maybe that shelf, like his knee, had long had invisible cracks, just waiting for a timing, a final weight, then collapsing with a loud crash.

If it was really like this, then who was responsible? The shelf? Or himself? Or this system that clearly knew he had old injuries, yet still let him move heavy objects?

He didn't know. He only knew, he was lying here now, body full of injuries, no money for treatment, tomorrow might be fired for "not being able to胜任 work."

Outside the window, sky gradually darkened. Sunset clouds dyed the sky blood-red.

Xiong Dali closed his eyes. Tired. Really tired.

## Scene-6 Faint Light in the Darkness

Dinnertime, Gou Laifu carried his lunch box to the infirmary to see him.

"Brother Dali, time to eat," Gou Laifu placed the lunch box on the bedside cabinet, opening it. Inside was porridge, buns, pickled vegetables, and that intact egg—the one Xiong Dali saved and didn't eat.

"Your egg," Gou Laifu said, "I reheated it for you."

Xiong Dali looked at that egg, egg white smooth, glowing with soft luster under the dim yellow light. He suddenly felt his nose sour, quickly turning his face away.

"What? Hurting badly?" Gou Laifu nervously asked.

Xiong Dali shook his head, turning back, accepting the lunch box. His hand was still shaking, couldn't hold chopsticks, could only use a spoon. Gou Laifu helped him break open the bun, soaking it in the porridge.

He slowly ate. Porridge was warm, bun softened, easy to swallow. He ate very slowly, very carefully, like completing a sacred ritual.

Halfway through eating, Bao Su came. In his hand was a plaster, black, with strong Chinese medicine smell.

"Put this on tonight," Bao Su placed the plaster on the bedside, "Promotes blood circulation and removes blood stasis."

Xiong Dali looked at that plaster, throat tightening. "Thanks," he said hoarsely.

"No need," Bao Su said, standing there looking at him for a while, then turned and left.

Niu Dazhuang also came, carrying a small cloth bag in his hand. "This is what we all chipped in," he placed the bag in Xiong Dali's hand, "Not much, just twenty lubi. You take it first, buy some medicine."

Xiong Dali gripped that cloth bag, rough fabric, coin outlines pressing into his palm. He wanted to say no, wanted to say you guys aren't well-off either, but words blocked in his throat, couldn't come out.

Finally he just nodded, gripping the bag tightly in his hand.

He thought of the instant of falling during the day, thought of that out-of-control falling feeling. Thought of the accident three and a half years ago, thought of those days and nights spent on the hospital bed. He fumbled out that egg from his shirt—the one Gou Laifu reheated for him, still warm now. He carefully peeled it, egg white smooth and intact. He broke it open, first eating egg white, then yolk. He wanted to think of other things—fish soup's freshness, plaster's medicinal fragrance, the weight of that twenty lubi in his hand, Gou Laifu's serious expression when saying "I reheated it for you."

Slowly, pain retreated to the background. Not that it didn't hurt, but he chose not to listen.

He fell asleep. Sleeping unsteadily, knee pain would occasionally stab him awake. But each time he woke, he could see that small lamp at the bedside, dim yellow halo, like a star in the darkness.

But tonight, just tonight, he allowed himself to temporarily forget.

Just tonight, he allowed himself, to just sleep.
